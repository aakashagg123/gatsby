Lesson 07

Launches, rollouts & migrations

It's Thursday, and the new AI drafting feature is ready. Marketing has a blog post scheduled for Monday, and someone suggests turning the feature on for everyone on Friday so it's "live" in time. Before you agree, there are a few questions to answer. If something is wrong, how many people will see it before you know? What's the one action that puts things back the way they were, and has anyone tried it? This lesson is about the machinery that makes those questions easy to answer.

Deploy is not release

Modern shipping separates two things that used to be one. Deploy means the code reaches production, switched off behind a feature flag. Release means users actually see it, at a percentage you control. That split is what makes progressive delivery possible. You go from internal users to a beta, then to a small slice of traffic, and then wider, checking metrics at each stage. The rollback is a flag flip rather than a fire drill.

Each stage exists to catch a different kind of problem.

  • Dark checks the integration in production with nobody exposed.
  • Internal use (dogfooding) gives you the cheapest honest users you'll ever have. If your own team routes around the feature, customers will too.
  • Beta users opted in and expect rough edges. Set expectations explicitly, because a beta user surprised by instability is a churn story.
  • Canary is a small slice of real traffic, say 1 to 5% as an example. It gives you real signal on crashes, latency, cost and, for AI, quality, while a disaster is still an apology rather than a headline. Write the abort criteria before you start, such as "flag off if the error rate passes X or acceptance drops by Y points". The middle of an incident is the worst time to negotiate a threshold.
  • Ramp watches the guardrail metrics at each step. Costs and slow-request tails that looked fine at 5% can behave very differently at 50%.
  • General availability (GA) means the flag comes out. A codebase full of permanent flags is tech debt on a timer, so set a removal date when you create the flag and give each flag an owner.

Rollback is a product requirement you own. Before any stage, ask what single action restores yesterday's behaviour, who can take it, and whether data written by the new version still works under the old one. That last question is the sneaky one. A feature that writes a new data format can make "just turn it off" impossible, and you want to find that out now rather than at 2 a.m.

Worked example: what a canary buys you

This example is invented, and so are its numbers. Your drafting feature will reach 200,000 daily users, each with about one session a day. Assume, for the illustration, that it ships with a bug that gives a bad draft in 1 session in 200, or 0.5%, which no offline test caught.

Exposure Users per day Bad sessions per day (0.5%)
Canary at 1% 2,000 10
5% 10,000 50
25% 50,000 250
Everyone at once 200,000 1,000

Each row is the share of 200,000 users, times 0.5%. Say the canary's abort criterion catches the problem after two days. By then 2 × 10 = 20 sessions have gone wrong. Turn it on for everyone on Friday instead, and the same two days produce 2 × 1,000 = 2,000 bad sessions. That's a hundred times as many, landing in the same weekend as the blog post.

The canary also changes who finds the bug. At 10 bad sessions a day, your acceptance-rate monitor or a beta customer finds it. At 1,000 a day, social media probably finds it first. The cost of going slowly is real, too. A one-week canary means the blog post either waits a week or announces a feature most readers can't see yet, and that's the trade you're choosing.

Launch is not release either

Release is traffic percentages, while launch is the coordinated moment you tell the world. You own the machinery for it.

  • Tiering. Not everything deserves fireworks. A top-tier launch with press and executive attention needs weeks of coordination, while a changelog note needs a paragraph. Deciding the tier early prevents both over-production and under-communication.
  • Support enablement. Support sees the surge first, so they get docs, known-issues lists and escalation paths before the announcement.
  • The go/no-go. This is a real checkpoint with a named owner, usually you, and a checklist covering feature complete, evals green, support ready, comms staged and rollback rehearsed. It also needs a genuine option of "no", since a go/no-go that has never said no is a ceremony.
  • The post-launch review. Book it for two to four weeks after launch, and compare the results with what the PRD predicted. Discovery to delivery covers why that review is what closes the loop.

Migrations: the transition is the project

Sooner or later you'll run a migration, such as a new billing system, a v2 API, re-platformed search or a swapped model provider. The pattern that survives contact with reality is expand, migrate, contract.

  1. Expand. Stand up the new path alongside the old one. Often both run at once, through dual writes or shadow mode, where the new system processes real traffic but its output is only logged and compared.
  2. Migrate. Move users in cohorts, easiest first, watching the comparisons. For external users this is where deprecation notices, timelines and incentives live. You're changing someone else's roadmap, and goodwill is spent or earned here.
  3. Contract. Turn the old path off and delete it. Everyone wants to skip this step, which leaves two systems to run, secure and reconcile forever. A migration is done when the old thing is off, so track that as the milestone rather than "new thing launched".

A model or prompt swap is a migration too, even when it looks like a one-line config change. It gets the same treatment: an offline eval diff, a shadow or side-by-side run, a staged ramp with quality gates, and a rollback that re-pins the old version. Technical product management for AI covers why, including the retirement notices providers give. In any migration, you own three questions. What breaks for which cohort, and do they know? What comparison proves the new path matches the old, and what is the rollback at each cohort? Someone also has to own the long tail, because the last few customers on the old path often take a large share of the effort.

A real failure: Knight Capital, August 2012

The US Securities and Exchange Commission's order on Knight Capital is one of the clearest public records of a deploy going wrong. Knight deployed new trading code to its eight order-routing servers over several days, and a technician didn't copy it to one of them. No second technician reviewed the deployment. The new code reused a flag that once switched on an old, unused function called Power Peg. Orders carrying that flag set off the old code on the eighth server.

While staff tried to find the problem in live trading, they uninstalled the new code from the seven servers where it had been deployed correctly. That made things worse, because incoming orders then set off the old code on those servers too. In about 45 minutes Knight built up an unintended multi-billion-dollar position, and it lost more than $460 million. For a PM the lesson isn't about trading. It's about a rollback nobody had rehearsed, a reused flag, and a deploy with no check that every server got the same code.

From the show: always roll out partially

Shuhao Zhang, who demonstrated an agent-run path to production on the show, is firm on staged rollout even when time is short:

"If you have the bandwidth and capacity, you should always do this partial rollout. Always. Even for a critical fix that, you know, you take an extra hour." — Shuhao Zhang, How to Ship to Production with AI Agents, The Growth Podcast, around 37:58

His argument is that partial rollout used to be costly because a person had to watch the numbers, and that agents can now do the watching. Whether or not you automate it, the extra hour is cheap next to the table above. Shobhit Chugh describes the other end of a product's life. His first product at Google came in through an acquisition, and he decided it had no future with Google Analytics already in the company:

"So now it's all about how do we get customers to migrate successfully and still have a good experience." — Shobhit Chugh, How to land your next PM role without working more, from Google PM turned Career Coach Shobhit Chugh, The Growth Podcast, around 20:19

That sentence is the whole migrate step. The decision to shut something down is the start of the work, and the customers' experience of moving is what they'll remember.

Where it goes wrong

  • The big-bang launch goes from 0 to 100% in one step and finds scale, cost and quality problems with the whole user base watching.
  • Rollback theatre is a rollback plan nobody rehearsed, which a data format change defeats the moment it's needed.
  • The flag graveyard is a codebase of permanent flags, multiplying test paths until nobody knows what production actually does.
  • The 95% migration leaves the old system up "temporarily" for years, doubling the operational surface.
  • The silent model swap changes a prompt or model with no eval diff and no staged ramp, and behaviour regresses for a workflow nobody thought to test.

What to do next

  • Separate deploy from release with flags, and know today's exposure percentage for each feature in flight.
  • Write the abort criteria for your current rollout, and decide whether a breach turns the flag off automatically or waits for a person.
  • Rehearse the rollback, including the question of whether new data works under the old version.
  • Brief support and publish docs before the announcement, not after.
  • Put a date on the contract step of your current migration, when the old thing goes off.
