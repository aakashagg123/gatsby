Lesson 01

The technical PM role

Last reviewed: 2026-11 · Volatility: medium

Your engineering lead pings you on a Tuesday afternoon. The team has two ways to build the new search feature. One answers in under a second but costs more to run, and the other is cheaper but takes three seconds. She wants to know which one to build. You could say "your call, you're the expert", or you could pick one because it sounds right. Both answers are wrong, and the reason they're wrong is most of what this lesson is about.

What you own, and what you don't

A technical product manager owns what every PM owns, which is outcomes. The difference is the kind of product. On platforms, APIs, data products and AI features, the hard decisions are technical. A PM who can't take part in them ends up watching the product get decided by default.

The cleanest way to hold the role is to sort decisions into three groups.

What the technical PM owns

Business, users, engineering feed the what and why · how and who stay engineering's

You decide what gets built, why, in what order, and what "good" means.

Business — viability, pricing, strategy
Users — needs, workflows, trust
Engineering — feasibility, cost, risk
What the technical PM owns
What & why — problem, outcome, priority
What good means — requirements, acceptance, metrics
How & who — architecture, staffing, estimates
owned by engineering, informed by the PM

You own the problem statement, the priority order and the requirements, including the non-functional ones such as speed and privacy. You also own the success metrics, the launch decision, and the communication that keeps everyone pointed at the same outcome.

You inform but don't own the architecture, the technology choices, the estimates and how the team organises its work. You bring constraints and consequences, such as "this must answer in under two seconds" or "this data can't leave the EU", and engineering brings the design.

You stay out of code review, task assignment and performance management. The quickest way to lose an engineering team is to do their jobs badly.

Back to the search feature. Choosing the architecture isn't your call, but the latency budget is, because it's a product decision about what users will tolerate. So the useful answer is a question back. What does three seconds do to people who search ten times a session, and what does the faster option cost per month at our volume? Once you've framed the tradeoff in product terms, the engineering choice usually makes itself.

Titles are a mess, so ask

Titles vary wildly between companies, though the underlying jobs don't.

Role Optimises for Customer is Typical outputs
Product manager User and business outcomes End users, buyers Strategy, PRDs, roadmaps
Technical PM Outcomes on technical products Often developers or internal teams PRDs with contracts, API specs, migration plans
Program manager (also "TPM" at some companies) Cross-team execution The organisation itself Plans, dependency maps, status
Engineering manager Team health and delivery The team Staffing, architecture reviews, career growth

The letters TPM can also mean technical program manager (TPM), which is an execution and dependency-management job rather than a product-ownership one. Ask what the letters mean before you interview, and read the job description rather than the title. Platform and API products also turn the empathy problem around, because your user is another engineer. Docs, error messages, versioning and the time it takes to make a first successful call become your user experience.

Where your influence comes from

You have no authority over the people who build the product, so your influence runs on three things you can supply.

  • Context. You know things the team can't see from inside the sprint: what customers said, what the business needs, what other teams are shipping. Deliver it steadily and decisions start going your way when you're not in the room.
  • Clarity. A crisp problem statement and an unambiguous definition of done are worth more than any number of meetings, since ambiguity is where a team's speed quietly drains away.
  • Trust. You earn it by learning enough of the technical picture to ask good questions, which is what the technical product sense track is for. You keep it by never negotiating an estimate you don't understand, and by absorbing ambiguity from above instead of passing panic down.

A picture that helps is to think of yourself as the team's interface to the rest of the company. Requests arrive in business language, you turn them into a prioritised, unambiguous contract, and the team's work flows back out through you as a story the company understands.

The job nobody else will do

One responsibility doesn't delegate, which is noticing incomplete work, your own included. Nobody downstream necessarily knows the right questions to ask. The tell is familiar. You send an answer already knowing the follow-up question you skipped, and it arrives ten minutes later. Once, that's a slip, but as a habit it's how products ship at a B-plus when everyone privately knew they could have been an A. The fix is structural, so before anything leaves your hands, ask what the reader will ask next and answer it in the same document.

Worked example: auditing your own week

This example is invented, and so are its numbers. Take an illustrative split of a technical PM's time, roughly 40/40/20 across three buckets. Now is unblocking the current build, next is discovery and definition for the next one or two bets, and later is strategy, metrics review, debt and the roadmap. It's a heuristic and not a research finding, but it gives you something to measure against.

Say you're the PM for a payments API and you log a 45-hour week by category.

Bucket What went in it Hours Share Rule-of-thumb share Rule-of-thumb hours
Now Standups, spec questions, two scope calls, a review against acceptance criteria 31 69% 40% 18
Next Two customer calls, a data pull 9 20% 40% 18
Later A metrics review 5 11% 20% 9
Total 45 100% 100% 45

The shares come from dividing each bucket by 45, so 31 / 45 is 69%, 9 / 45 is 20% and 5 / 45 is 11%. The rule-of-thumb hours are 40%, 40% and 20% of 45, which gives 18, 18 and 9. You're spending 13 hours more than the guide on now, and 9 hours less on next.

Nothing in that week was wasted, which is what makes the pattern dangerous. If it holds for more than a sprint or two, the next build starts without definition. The team then spends its sprints asking you questions you could have answered in advance. That pushes now even higher. The fix here is concrete. You move two recurring status meetings to a written update, which frees about four hours, and you put two fixed discovery blocks in your calendar for the next bet. Even that only gets next to 13 hours, so the remaining gap is a conversation with your engineering lead about which scope questions the tech lead can answer without you.

The technical PM interview

Technical PM interviews often include a round that product-sense interviews don't have: system or API design, done from a PM's altitude rather than an engineer's. (For the general interview craft this round builds on, see communication in product sense.)

The interviewer isn't grading your architecture. They're watching whether you ask what the data looks like, what scale you're working at and what you're optimising for before you draw anything. A candidate who opens with "who calls this API, how often, and what happens if it's down?" is doing the job. One who jumps straight to a diagram is auditioning for a different seat.

A frame that holds up goes like this. State the goal and the users, then name the non-functional requirements that matter here, such as latency, consistency and who owns which data. Sketch the contract rather than the implementation, and say the tradeoffs out loud, the same way you'd read an RFC. Then connect each technical choice to a product consequence. A sentence like "we'd need eventual consistency here, so a user could briefly see a stale balance, and is that acceptable?" marks a technical PM answer. The same frame works for AI versions of the round, on eval design, retrieval and serving. The extra layer there is reasoning about what happens when the model is wrong.

From the show: why "technical" became the default

Lewis C. Lin, who coaches candidates for PM interviews, describes a shift in what hiring managers expect:

"kind of an unspoken belief that a more effective product manager is a technical product manager. And it kind of makes sense because one of your key stakeholders is engineers." — Lewis C. Lin, 500,000 Professionals Have Used His Book To Crack Interviews at Big Companies, The Growth Podcast, around 38:57

That's his read of the hiring market, not a measured trend, but it matches why this track exists. Mike Davey adds a practical warning about the title itself:

"I applied to a job that was a technical product manager. But the actual job description sounded like a head of product role." — Mike Davey, How He Became a Series C VP of Product in 10 Years: Mike Davey, The Growth Podcast, around 01:09:08

His point is to read the description, the company stage and the hiring pattern, and to trust those over the title. The same letters can mean a platform owner, a program manager or a company's first product hire.

Where it goes wrong

  • The backseat architect overrules technical designs they half understand. Engineers stop bringing real options and start managing the PM instead.
  • The ticket clerk writes down whatever the loudest stakeholder or the tech lead wants and adds no judgment, even though the judgment is the whole value of the role.
  • The absentee hands off "the technical stuff" wholesale and is then surprised by a latency, cost or privacy property that was knowable months earlier.
  • The hero translator routes all information through themselves. It feels like influence, but it's a bottleneck that stops the day they go on holiday.

What to do this week

  • Write the outcome your product owes the business, and the problem it solves for users, in one sentence each.
  • Draft a list of decisions that are yours, theirs and shared, and walk your engineering lead through it.
  • Name the two or three technical constraints that shape your roadmap most, such as a latency budget, a data boundary or a dependency.
  • Log one week of your time against now, next and later, and book discovery time if next is thin.
  • Recall the last time an engineer changed your mind with a better option, and make a point of asking for one this week.
