Lesson 09

Technical product management for AI

An email arrives from your model provider. The model your feature is pinned to will be retired, and there's a newer one they recommend. Your engineering lead asks whether you should switch now, and the room splits. One engineer says the new model is better on every benchmark. A second remembers the last upgrade broke a customer's workflow, and nobody can say what the switch would do to your product. That debate is the symptom this capstone treats. When you can answer "should we upgrade?" with a score by the end of the day, most of the AI-specific craft is in place.

Everything still applies, bent the same way

The previous eight lessons taught the general craft. Every one of those practices still applies to an AI product, and they all bend for the same reason. Behaviour is probabilistic, and quality is something you discover rather than design. So the operating loop reorganises around evidence.

Read it as three connected loops. The inner loop, build and eval, runs daily, and no prompt, retrieval or model change lands without a score. The release loop, from eval through rollout to production, runs weekly or monthly, with quality gating the ramp. The outer loop, from production back to the spec, runs quarterly, as what users actually do reshapes what "good" means. Your job as PM is to make sure all three arrows back into the eval suite actually exist.

Eval-driven development

The eval suite is to an AI feature what the test suite is to code, except that the PM co-owns it, because it encodes product judgment. In eval-driven development, a graded example set is the spec, the regression suite and the launch gate at once.

  • Sourcing. Start with real or realistic inputs from discovery, enough to cover the main kinds of request and the failure cases you already know about. Grade the ideal outputs with a rubric. This is the graded-examples section of your PRD, turned into infrastructure.
  • Grading. There are three tiers, in cost order. Programmatic checks ask simple questions such as whether the output is valid JSON or cites a source. Model-graded rubrics use a model to judge tone or groundedness, which is cheap and scales but needs spot-checks against people. Human review is the gold standard, spent where stakes are highest.
  • Judges. Hamel Husain and Shreya Shankar's evals FAQ recommends binary pass/fail labels over 1-to-5 scales, and a separate judge for each failure mode. It also recommends checking each judge against human labels, for both real failures caught and good outputs passed. For error analysis, it suggests reviewing at least 100 traces, and continuing past that while you're still learning.
  • The bar. You set a launch threshold as the PM, such as "at least 90% acceptable, zero harmful". Where the bar sits is a product decision that balances user trust, the cost of errors and competitive urgency.
  • Maintenance. A static suite rots as users find new ways to use and break the feature. Feed production failures back in on a schedule, retire cases that no longer tell options apart, and give someone ownership of the suite's freshness.
  • The ritual. Hold a standing PM and engineering session that reads raw production traces together, labels failures in free text, and clusters them before deciding which ones deserve an automated evaluator. A PM–engineer disagreement about whether an output passes is a requirement being discovered, not noise.

Once the suite exists, hard conversations get easier. "Can we ship?" becomes a score, "did the new model help?" becomes a diff, and comparing two prompts takes an afternoon instead of a meeting.

What each lesson's craft gains

  • Discovery (lesson 2). Feasibility is a scored spike, run before the bet enters delivery. Add two viability checks. One is unit economics, where cost scales with tokens times volume at realistic context sizes. The other is data rights, meaning whether you can use this data for this purpose, feedback capture included.
  • Specs (lesson 3). Acceptance criteria become eval thresholds, behavioural bounds and budgets. Spec the failure experience too, since what the user sees when the model is wrong, slow or down is product surface.
  • Prioritisation (lesson 4). Discount confidence until a spike has run, reserve capacity for eval and data work, and use effective reach, which is reach times the share of inputs the feature handles well.
  • Execution (lesson 5). Experiment-shaped work runs as time-boxed spikes with score-based exit criteria.
  • Metrics (lesson 6). Offline eval scores pair with online proxies such as acceptance, edit distance and retry rate, and cost per request joins latency on the guardrail list.
  • Releases (lesson 7). Every model or prompt change is a migration. It gets an eval diff, a shadow or side-by-side run, a staged ramp with quality gates, and a rollback that re-pins the old version. That rollback is cheap only if you versioned them.
  • Incidents (lesson 8). Quality drops are pageable events, and every feature ships with a kill switch and a degraded mode you chose on purpose.

Worked example: the retirement email

This example is invented, and so are its numbers. Your support assistant is pinned to a model that the provider says will retire in 60 days, which is the minimum notice Anthropic commits to for publicly released models. You run the 300-case eval suite on the current model and on the recommended replacement.

Slice Cases Current model passes Candidate passes
Refund-policy questions 40 38 33
Everything else 260 232 246
All cases 300 270 (90%) 279 (93%)

The candidate wins overall, 279 against 270, which is 93% against 90%. Look at the slices, though. It gains 246 − 232 = 14 cases on everything else and loses 38 − 33 = 5 on refund policy. Refund answers are the ones most likely to cost you money. A config-change upgrade would have shipped a regression that the headline number hid.

So you plan it as a migration. You give the team two weeks to fix the refund regression in the prompt and re-run the diff. Then come two weeks of shadow running on live traffic, and two weeks of staged ramp. That's 14 + 14 + 14 = 42 days, which leaves 60 − 42 = 18 days of slack before the old model stops answering. Eighteen days is not much if the prompt fix takes a second round, so you start this week rather than at the next planning cycle. The cost is that six weeks of the team's time go to an upgrade nobody asked for, and that belongs on the roadmap as a line item.

Context, flywheels and control points

An important question for an AI feature's quality is what context the model needs to make the decision well, more than what data you happen to have. A clever prompt on its own answers that badly, since it breaks under rephrasing and carries no memory between calls. Your job is to specify the context a decision requires, the same way a spec sets an eval threshold. Context engineering for the product leader covers this in full.

The compounding asset of an AI product usually isn't the model, because competitors can rent the same one. It's the data flywheel, where usage produces captured feedback, which improves the evals, which makes iteration safer and faster, which improves the product and brings more usage. Two PM jobs make it spin. Instrument feedback capture (accepted, edited, abandoned) as a launch requirement, and secure the right to learn from it through the privacy policy, enterprise contracts and regional law. A competitor with the same model and a faster flywheel tends to pull ahead.

The flywheel's strategic twin is workflow capture, which means owning a control point, the workflow step whose outputs the downstream work depends on. When you weigh an adjacent workflow, ask which way integration would run if another company captured it. Would they integrate into you, or you into them?

The long game

Some roadmap truths only bite in a product's second year, so budget for them in its first.

  • Models drift on your watch. Data shifts, user behaviour changes in response to the product, and providers ship new versions. Re-evaluation and periodic re-prompting or re-pinning are a permanent line item, not one-off maintenance.
  • Pinned models get retired. Pinning protects you from silent change but not from retirement. Anthropic gives at least 60 days' notice before it retires a publicly released model. OpenAI gives at least 6 months for generally available models, unless safety or compliance concerns require a faster timeline, and at least 3 months for specialised variants and image models. Preview models can go with much shorter notice, such as two weeks. Request parameters change too. Anthropic's docs list temperature, top_p and top_k as removed in API version 2026-10-21. Put each retirement date on the roadmap, and check the provider's deprecation page, because dates and notice periods change.
  • Ethical debt compounds like tech debt. Bias audits, explainability, privacy reviews and fairness checks deferred at launch don't get cheaper. They get baked into data, behaviour and user expectations, so schedule them with the same discipline as debt paydown, before an incident schedules them for you.
  • Platforms outlast features. The investments that compound are rarely the visible features. They're the eval infrastructure, data pipelines and feedback instrumentation every later feature rides on.

Vibe coding: the prototype is the new spec

AI coding tools such as Claude Code and Cursor have made working software cheap enough that PMs build it themselves. Andrej Karpathy coined the name for it, "vibe coding", and Collins chose it as its word of the year for 2025. That changes the operating loop in three ways.

  • Discovery ships artifacts, not documents. A working prototype in front of five users teaches more than a PRD in front of five stakeholders. Increasingly the prototype is the spec, and the written document records the decisions the prototype forced.
  • The PM–engineering contract gets shorter. "Here's what I mean" becomes something engineers can click, which removes a whole class of ambiguity. It also adds a duty to say plainly that the prototype shows intent, not architecture.
  • The new failure mode is prototype-grade code in production. Vibe-coded software demos well and hides its corners, such as missing error handling, tenancy and evals. Prototypes are for learning, and the eval-gated pipeline above is for shipping.

Use these tools in your own work, too. Generating mock-up variations, clustering bug reports, critiquing a flow and drafting the first pass of a document are cheap training in where models shine and where they quietly fail. The eval bar and the scoping calls above run on that intuition.

Where it goes wrong

  • Demo-driven development iterates until the demo impresses, ships, and then finds real inputs look nothing like the script.
  • Eval-less iteration ships prompt changes on feel, so every change is a coin flip you can't tell apart from progress.
  • The static eval is built at launch and never fed. A year later it certifies quality on problems users stopped having.
  • Flywheel-blind economics ships without feedback capture, or without the rights to use it, and then wonders why the product stopped improving.
  • Treating the model as the moat bets differentiation on model choice while a competitor with the same model out-learns you on data and iteration speed.
  • The surprise retirement leaves a pinned model's end date off the roadmap until the email arrives, and then there's no time for an eval diff.

What to do next

  • Give every AI feature you own an eval suite with a PM-set bar, and score every prompt or model change against it before it ships.
  • Pin and version prompts and models, so a rollback is a re-pin.
  • Write down the context each feature needs, and where it's retrieved or remembered from.
  • Instrument accept, edit and abandon signals, and confirm you have the right to learn from them.
  • Put every pinned model's retirement date, or its earliest possible one, on the roadmap.
  • Sketch the three loops for your product, and mark the arrow that's broken today.
