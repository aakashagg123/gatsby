Lesson 08

Incidents & postmortems

It's 2:40 on a Wednesday afternoon, and support leads are messaging you that the AI assistant is telling customers they can get refunds the policy doesn't allow. Engineering is already looking at logs. Someone in the channel suggests waiting until they understand the cause before touching anything, and someone else asks whether they should post on the status page. Everyone is looking at you, because none of these questions is really technical. Owning a product includes owning its worst hour, and this lesson is about what you do in it and afterwards.

Respond, recover, learn

An incident is any unplanned event that degrades something users depend on. The work around it has three phases with different tempos. Respond means stopping the damage, recover means restoring service and then trust, and learn means a postmortem that turns causes into funded fixes.

Severity drives everything: a SEV1 gets a war room; a SEV3 gets a ticket. Agree on the ladder before you need it.

Severity drives everything. A top-severity incident, often called a SEV1, is one where users are broadly down or data is at risk, and it gets a war room and executive updates. A SEV3, a degraded corner case, gets a ticket. Agree the ladder before you need it, because arguing about severity during an incident is how minutes become hours.

Two rules hold across every framework. The first is to mitigate before you diagnose. Roll back first and find the root cause later, which is why rollback-ready releases are an incident tool as much as a launch tool. The second is to have one incident commander, a single person directing the response, so ten helpful engineers don't make eleven uncoordinated changes.

Your job while it's happening

The PM is rarely the one typing the fix. Your job is the blast radius around it.

  • Size the user impact. Who is affected, how badly, and is it getting worse? Engineering knows what's broken, and you know what it means in customers, commitments and revenue.
  • Own the communication. Keep it honest, plain and on a cadence, through the status page, support macros and account teams for the biggest customers. "We know, we're on it, next update at half past" beats both silence and spin, because trust is lost less by the outage than by the handling.
  • Make the product calls. Degrade or disable? Ship the risky fast fix or the safe slow one? These are product decisions that arrive dressed as technical ones, which is why being in the room is the job.
  • Keep the timeline. Someone should log what happened when. It's the raw material of the postmortem, and it can't be rebuilt from memory afterwards.

Worked example: the refund incident

This example is invented, and so are its numbers. At 14:00, a prompt change for the support assistant ships to all traffic at once. Normally agents override or correct 8% of the assistant's answers. By 14:40 the override rate over the last 30 minutes is 21%, which crosses the alert threshold and pages the on-call engineer.

At 14:45 an incident commander is named and the incident is set at the middle severity. One feature is giving wrong answers, but nothing is down and no data is lost. You argue for mitigation first, and at 14:52 the team re-pins the previous prompt version. The override rate is back to normal within twenty minutes.

Now the sizing. The bad prompt was live from 14:00 to 14:52, which is 52 minutes. The assistant handles about 60 conversations a minute, so 52 × 60 = 3,120 conversations saw the new prompt. The override rate rose from 8% to 21%, an excess of 13 points, and 3,120 × 0.13 is about 406. Since the rate climbed over the window rather than jumping at 14:00, treat 406 as a rough upper estimate of the conversations that got a wrong answer.

That number drives the product call you make next. You decide to honour any refund the assistant promised in that window, and support sends a correction to the affected customers. The cost is some refunds the policy wouldn't normally allow, and in return you don't spend the next month arguing with customers who were told something by your own product.

The postmortem finds three causes, not one. The prompt change shipped without an eval diff, it went to all traffic instead of a canary, and the alert looked at a 30-minute window, which added detection time. Each becomes an action item with an owner and a date. Prompt changes must pass the refund-policy eval cases before merge. Prompt changes ship through the same staged ramp as code. And the alert window drops to ten minutes.

Blameless postmortems

A postmortem starts from the premise that people acted reasonably on the information they had, and that the system let them down. That's a practical choice as much as a kind one. The moment a postmortem can hurt someone, it stops hearing the truth.

Decide the triggers in advance. The chapter on postmortem culture in Google's site reliability engineering (SRE) book lists five common ones. They are user-visible downtime or degradation beyond a threshold, data loss of any kind, an on-call intervention such as a rollback, a resolution time above a threshold, and a monitoring failure that meant a person found the problem. Any stakeholder can also ask for one. The same chapter is blunt about review: "An unreviewed postmortem might as well never have existed."

Keep the document short and structured. It covers the impact (users, duration, cost), the timeline, and the root causes, plural, found by asking why repeatedly until you reach conditions rather than the trigger. A deploy is usually the spark, while the missing alert, the unbounded retry and the single point of failure are the fuel. It also covers what went well, and action items with owners and dates. That last part is where postmortems go to die, so track the items like features and review them monthly. A repeat incident with an unshipped action item is a process failure. And a stack of postmortems pointing at the same subsystem is the best input you'll get for prioritising reliability work.

What changes for AI features

  • Quality incidents are incidents. Define them in advance, such as an eval score below the bar in production sampling, a spike in overrides, or a guardrail firing at ten times its usual rate. Wire them to drift detection so a drop pages someone instead of piling up quietly.
  • Every AI feature ships with a kill switch and a chosen degraded mode. That might mean falling back to the previous model or prompt, to retrieval-only answers, or to the human process. Turning it off should be a decision made calmly in advance, not invented at 2 a.m.
  • A model or prompt change is a change event. Treat it with the pin-and-diff discipline, so the rollback is a re-pin.
  • The screenshot incident is its own class, where one appalling output goes viral. Capture the trace, reproduce it, add a guardrail for the pattern, and add the case to the eval suite so it can't ship again unnoticed.

Quality incidents can hide for weeks. In September 2025, Anthropic published a postmortem on three infrastructure bugs that intermittently degraded Claude's responses between August and early September of that year. Its own evaluations didn't capture the degradation users were reporting, and its privacy controls limited how engineers could look at the conversations involved. Anthropic said it would make its evaluations more sensitive and run quality evaluations continuously on production systems. For a PM the takeaway is that an offline suite that passes isn't a detector. Budget for production sampling, and make sure feedback reports reach someone who can act on them.

When there's a legal clock

Some AI incidents carry a reporting deadline. Under Article 73 of the EU AI Act, a provider of a high-risk AI system must report a serious incident to the market surveillance authority. The deadline is no later than 15 days after becoming aware of it, or 10 days if a person died. For a widespread infringement or a serious disruption of critical infrastructure, it's 2 days. Article 73 also says the provider must not alter the system in a way that could affect the later investigation of the cause before telling the authorities.

The Act originally applied its main high-risk rules from 2 August 2026. The Digital Omnibus on AI, Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, moved them. They now apply from 2 December 2027 for stand-alone high-risk systems (Annex III), and from 2 August 2028 for AI in products covered by the laws in Annex I. As published in the Official Journal, the Omnibus doesn't change Article 73's deadlines. Whether and when the duty applies to your system is a question for legal counsel. Your part is to agree in advance who decides that an incident is "serious", and to make sure the incident runbook doesn't destroy the evidence.

From the show: detection and honesty

Jack Hirsch, Okta's VP of product management and AI when the episode was recorded, makes the point that incidents aren't a sign of a bad team:

"every organization of any consequence has breaches. Spoiler alert, everyone gets breached. Now, it's how you detect it, how fast you respond, what your internal and public responses are [...]" — Jack Hirsch, The AI PM's Guide to Security - with Okta's VP of PM & AI, Jack Hirsch, The Growth Podcast, around 37:44

He's talking about security breaches, but the frame carries over to any incident. Detection, speed and the honesty of your response are what users judge. Maria Cuasay adds a warning about the postmortem meeting itself:

"So you can have like one big meeting. Most of the time you won't actually get anything out of it. People are kind of like too shy to talk." — Maria Cuasay, How to Build Things Faster as a Product Team, The Growth Podcast, around 37:18

Her fix is smaller meetings and one-to-one conversations with the people who went quiet. That's the blameless principle in practice, since the truth tends to come out where people feel safe.

Where it goes wrong

  • Diagnosis before mitigation spends an hour finding the root cause while the rollback button waits, and users pay for the team's curiosity.
  • The headless incident has no commander, so parallel fixes collide and one of them causes the second incident.
  • Blameful postmortems make the room optimise for self-defence, and the same incident comes back with a different name on it.
  • The action-item graveyard documents lessons and never funds them.
  • Availability-only monitoring shows the AI feature as up while its answers are wrong, with no error, no page and no idea.

What to do next

  • Write a severity ladder with your engineering lead, and name who commands a top-severity incident.
  • For each critical feature, write down the rollback and the date it was last exercised.
  • Give one named person the job of user communication during incidents, with a set cadence.
  • Review open postmortem action items monthly, and escalate any repeat incident.
  • For each AI feature, make a quality drop pageable and choose its degraded mode.
  • If you might sell a high-risk AI system in the EU, ask counsel who decides an incident is serious and what your reporting runbook says.
