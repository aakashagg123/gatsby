Lesson 04

Creativity: from strategic thinking to creative execution

Every competitor in your market has access to the same models you do. If your roadmap says "add AI to onboarding", so does theirs, and the demo you're proud of could ship from three other companies next quarter. So the creative question is no longer whether you can build the feature. It is what problem you point it at, for whom, and what you'll refuse to build so that the bet gets your full attention.

Creativity in product work has two halves. Strategic thinking decides where the product plays, which covers positioning, segments, differentiation and, above all, focus. Creative execution is the day-to-day generation of new solutions inside that strategy, through mindset, team rituals and techniques. Strategy without execution is a nice deck, and execution without strategy is motion without direction. Creativity tends to work best inside strategic guardrails.

Strategy: where the product plays

This lesson only sketches strategy, because Markets and strategy and Competitive advantage and strategy cover it in depth. The short version is four moves.

  • Positioning. Fill in "For [target segment] who [need], [Product] is a [category] that [benefit]. Unlike [competition], it [unique differentiator]." Good positioning is specific. "A task app for working parents that reduces mental load by prioritising intelligently" guides decisions in a way that "a task app for everyone" never will.
  • Segmentation. Choose whom to serve, ideally by need or behaviour rather than demographics. Slack began as the internal chat tool of Tiny Speck, a game studio, which is a need real users already had, proven inside one team before it became the product.
  • Differentiation. Users pick you for something new (a capability others lack) or something better (the same need met faster, cheaper or more pleasantly). Either way it has to matter to the user, because a unique feature nobody cares about wins nothing.
  • Focus. Be clear on what you will and won't do. Derive a few pillars from your vision and use them to judge ideas, so that an idea which doesn't strengthen a pillar gets shelved, however good the demo.

Roger Martin has a test for whether any of this is real:

"strategy is what you do, not what you say." — Roger Martin, Product Strategy Masterclass from Global Authority on Strategy, The Growth Podcast, around 14:45

By his test, your strategy is whatever your last three launches add up to, whatever the strategy document claims. Strategy for an AI feature works the same way. If a competitor shipped the same model tomorrow, what would still make your product the one users choose? If the honest answer is "nothing", you have a feature, not a position.

Instagram is the classic story of focus. Its founders first built Burbn, a location-based app, and Kevin Systrom later wrote that the iPhone version "felt cluttered, and overrun with features". They cut it back to sharing, commenting on and liking photos, and the app that came out of that in 2010 was Instagram, built around photo sharing and a set of filters. The creative act was the cut. If you can't name the last good idea you shelved because it didn't strengthen a pillar, you have a backlog rather than a strategy.

From the show: proven, better, new

Mark Pincus, the founder of Zynga, sorts every part of a new product into three bins. Proven is what already works for this audience, better is where you improve on it, and new is your genuinely novel idea. His view of the first bin is blunt:

"anything that's proven, you are gonna legally copy And the better product maker you are, the better you are at copying." — Mark Pincus, How to steal your way to a hit product, with Mark Pincus (founder, Zynga), The Growth Podcast, around 16:05

The reason is to protect the third bin. In his words, "New is your new novel idea, your innovation zone", and he wants it isolated "so that you don't fail for the wrong reasons" (around 17:09). If a launch mixes a novel idea with a clumsy version of everything users already expect, you can't tell whether the idea failed or the basics did. Copying has legal and ethical limits, and he says "legally" for a reason, but the sorting habit carries over to any product.

Execution: generating ideas inside the rails

Even the best strategy stalls without the day-to-day creativity to carry it out.

Mindset. Creativity starts with psychological safety and a growth mindset. People have to feel safe proposing wild ideas and asking "stupid" questions, so model it yourself. Celebrate experiments that fail, treat setbacks as learning, and reward the people who iterate. Bring design, engineering, data and marketing into ideation early, because each lens produces ideas the others won't.

Rituals make creativity a habit rather than an accident.

  • Brainstorms with rules ("defer judgment", "no idea too crazy"), plus formats like Crazy Eights (8 sketches in 8 minutes) and brainwriting (write silently, then share), so quieter people contribute as much as louder ones.
  • Design sprints. GV (formerly Google Ventures) describes its sprint as a five-day process that goes from mapping a problem to testing a realistic prototype with customers. Jake Knapp began running sprints at Google in 2010. Even a mini cycle of brainstorm, prototype and test adds structure.
  • Hack days, with freedom to build anything product-related. Gmail's undo send shows how an experiment can graduate. It started in 2009 as an experimental Gmail Labs feature and became a standard setting in 2015.
  • "How might we…?" framing and the 5 Whys, which reframe a problem from its root and open new solution spaces.

Techniques help you break out of a rut.

Technique What it does
SCAMPER Substitute, Combine, Adapt, Modify, Put to another use, Eliminate, Reverse — systematically tweak an existing flow
Mind mapping Branch outward from a central problem to surface non-linear connections
Analogy thinking Borrow structure from a far field ("how do airports manage flow?")
Constraint brainstorming Add an absurd constraint ("zero eng resources," "voice only") to force new approaches
User co-creation Ask power users how they'd solve it

Not every idea will be good. The point is to generate range, then filter it against the strategy.

AI prototyping as divergence

AI tools have made the range part cheap. Where a team used to sketch three options and build one, a PM can now generate several working prototypes in an afternoon. Sachin Rekhi argues for using that power to widen the options rather than to rush to one answer:

"AI shouldn't be used just to create one output. We should be using it to create multiple outputs, just the way we'd use a designer." — Sachin Rekhi, Advanced Guide to AI Prototyping with Sachin Rekhi (Reforge), The Growth Podcast, around 09:36

The discipline is to diverge, test and then decide. Generate several genuinely different prototypes, put each in front of a few users, and choose on evidence. The trap is falling for the first prototype because it works, which turns a cheap exploration into an expensive commitment. The engineering side of this, including why prototype code shouldn't go to production, is in Technical product management for AI.

Worked example: three onboarding prototypes

This example is invented, and so are the numbers. Say you're the PM for a project-management app, and new teams struggle to set up their first project. In one afternoon you use an AI prototyping tool to build three different versions.

  • A, a chat assistant that asks questions and builds the project as you answer.
  • B, a template picker whose chosen template the model fills in from a one-line description.
  • C, an import that turns an existing spreadsheet into a project.

You sort each one with Pincus's bins. B is mostly proven (templates are a familiar pattern) with a "better" layer on top. C is the only genuinely new idea, and A is new too but in a way users haven't asked for. Then you give each prototype to five people from your target segment and ask them to set up a real project.

Prototype Finished setup (of 5) Median minutes Asked to keep using it (of 5)
A, chat assistant 2 11 1
B, template picker 4 4 3
C, spreadsheet import 3 6 4

B wins on completion and speed, while C wins on pull, with four of five asking to keep using it. So you ship B as the default path, because it is proven and better, and you put C behind a single "import a spreadsheet" button as the isolated new bet. If C underperforms, you'll know the import idea failed rather than the whole onboarding. You drop A, even though it made the best demo, because two of five finishing is a signal you can't argue with. Five users per prototype is a small sample, so you treat the result as a direction and confirm it with the launch metrics.

Where it goes wrong

  • "Add AI" as strategy. A capability with no segment, positioning or differentiation behind it. You see it as a launch with good press and flat usage.
  • Differentiation nobody wants. Unique features that don't map to a real user need.
  • No focus. A strategy that never says "no", so effort scatters across a long roadmap.
  • Novelty everywhere. Every part of the product is new, so when it fails nobody can say why.
  • Falling for the first prototype. AI makes one prototype easy, and the team stops exploring the moment it works.
  • Ideation theatre. Brainstorms that generate sticky notes but never reach a prototype or a user.

What to do next

  • Write the product's positioning in the "for [who] who [need]… unlike…" form, and test it on someone outside the team.
  • List your last three launches and ask Roger Martin's question: what strategy do they add up to?
  • Name the last good idea you shelved because it didn't strengthen a pillar.
  • Sort your next feature into proven, better and new, and keep the new part isolated.
  • Next time you prototype with AI, build at least three different versions and test them before choosing.
  • Adopt one repeatable ritual this quarter, such as a hack day or a monthly idea round.
