Lesson 06

Domain expertise: turning knowledge into intuition

Last reviewed: 2026-11 · Volatility: medium

You've just joined a company that sells software to hospitals, or to banks, or to freight brokers. In your first planning meeting someone proposes a feature, and everyone else in the room can tell within seconds that compliance will never approve it. You can't, because you don't yet know the world the product lives in. PM fundamentals transfer across industries, but domain expertise, deep knowledge of the specific world your users work in, is what lets you tell a must-have from a nice-to-have before anyone has to explain it.

For an AI PM there are two domains at once. One is the user's industry. The other is the AI domain itself, which covers what models do well, where they make things up, and what evaluation and safety require. Thin knowledge of either produces confident, wrong product bets. The most expensive version is an AI feature that's impressive in the demo and harmful in the real workflow, because nobody understood what a wrong answer costs there.

Domain expertise

The trap is "I am the user." The antidote is the outsider's question, kept alive on purpose.

Why knowing the domain changes your judgment

Picture a PM building software for clinics (an illustration). With thin domain knowledge, they build an electronic medical record (EMR) around generic UX and obvious needs like notes and scheduling. With deep knowledge, they know the details that matter. There are compliance rules such as the US Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA), and doctors have seconds to enter data between patients. There are common billing problems, and there are the politics of adopting a new tool. That knowledge becomes a filter. It tells you which requests are must-haves, which ideas will meet resistance, and what truly adds value in that context.

Experts also decide faster because they recognise patterns and recall what happened in similar situations. A fintech PM who knows payments well will quickly reject a "cool" feature whose compliance approval would take a year. Then they'll look for a path that meets the need without the red tape. A newcomer tends to charge in and hit the wall.

Abhishek Fatehpuria, who builds AI products in fintech, describes what that wall looks like from the inside:

"you need to like really understand the regulatory landscape you're working in. Because if you don't understand that landscape, you're going to go to your legal partners [...] and they're going to think you're kind of crazy." — Abhishek Fatehpuria, How to Build AI products in FinTech | $100B Lessons from Robinhood VP PM, The Growth Podcast, around 08:08

Domain knowledge also builds credibility. Speaking your users' and stakeholders' language makes you more persuasive, and sales and marketing back your calls when they can see you understand the market.

The expert's trap

Domain expertise has to complement the rest of product sense, not replace it. Marty Cagan's warning is that "it can be dangerous for a product manager to have too much domain expertise," because experts come to believe they are "more like their target customer than they really are". He also writes that deep domain expertise is truly essential for only a few products. In his view, strong PMs can come up to speed on most domains quickly if they approach it aggressively. His remedy is that the PM "needs to constantly revisit assumptions about the domain and the customers", because new regulations, behaviours and competitors will surprise you.

Here's an illustration of the trap, not a sourced case. Imagine a records system for clinicians whose design input came mostly from billing and compliance experts. It handles claims perfectly and makes a doctor click through screen after screen to record an ordinary visit. Domain expertise was present, but it was the wrong domain's, and nobody asked the outsider's question of why a doctor's tool feels like an invoice. Expertise tells you how the world works, while only contact with users tells you whose version of that world your product just encoded.

A newcomer's "why is it done this way at all?" sometimes becomes a breakthrough, precisely because they don't accept "that's just how it is". As you gain expertise, keep that outsider curiosity alive. Rotate people across domains, keep validating with real users, and treat the domain as something that keeps moving.

From the show: learn fast, or arrive knowing?

Practitioners on The Growth Podcast disagree here, and the disagreement is useful. Marty Cagan's own start as a PM was built on direct contact rather than prior knowledge:

"I remember the person who was coaching me as a new product manager would not let me make a single decision until I visited in person 30 customers, 15 in the US, 15 in Europe." — Marty Cagan, The Marty Cagan Episode: Product Management Crash Course in 61 Minutes, The Growth Podcast, around 25:49

Those are his words as the transcript records them, and the numbers may be misheard. The point stands either way, which is that the expertise came from visiting customers. George Harter puts more weight on arriving with the domain already in hand:

"So right now, I would say it's more important than ever to have industry experience and product management expertise." — George Harter, The Ultimate Guide to Knowing Your Users as a PM, The Growth Podcast, around 07:26

Harter is talking about getting hired in a crowded job market, where industry experience gets your application through the filter. Cagan is talking about doing the job well once you're there. Both can be true. If you're switching domains, expect the hiring side to be harder, and once you're in, learn the way Cagan's coach made him learn.

Building expertise without getting trapped

When you enter a new domain, be systematic about it.

  • Learn intensively, with a time limit. In the first months, read the industry reports, the regulations and the trade press. Cagan writes that it generally takes him one to three months to come up to speed on a new domain, and that some people are faster and some slower.
  • Ask the "dumb" questions. Have experts explain things to you. If you don't understand how an Automated Clearing House (ACH) payment differs from a wire transfer, ask an operations manager. It's far better to ask early than to decide wrong out of ignorance.
  • Get hands-on. Watch hospital staff use the software, sell something on your own e-commerce platform, or build a small project with your own API. Domain knowledge is procedural and contextual, and the vocabulary is the smallest part of it.
  • Build a network. Part of expertise is knowing where to get answers fast, so find your go-to people (the veteran engineer, the candid customer) and bring in specialists in legal, compliance and operations early.

Worked example: a 90-day domain plan

This plan is invented, and so are its numbers. Say you've moved from a consumer app to a business payments company, to lead an AI feature that helps operations teams handle disputed card transactions. You budget about six hours a week for deliberate learning, which over 13 weeks is roughly 78 hours.

Phase Goal Concrete actions Done when
Days 1–30 Learn the language Read the card network's dispute rules summary and two industry reports. Sit with the operations team for two full days. Start a glossary. The glossary has 40 terms and an ops lead says it's right
Days 31–60 Learn the workflow and the stakes Hold 10 customer conversations about the last dispute each handled. Shadow one compliance review. Write the five hardest questions a customer could ask, and answer them. You can explain what a wrong AI answer costs, in money and in deadlines
Days 61–90 Make a domain-informed call Hold 10 more customer conversations. Write a one-page decision memo on where the model may suggest and where a person must decide. The memo survives review by compliance and by two customers

The plan adds up to 20 customer conversations, two days of shadowing and one compliance review. The "done when" column matters more than the actions, because it turns learning into a checkable result and stops it sliding into endless reading. The memo at the end is what an expert would produce, a judgment about where a model's mistakes are tolerable, and it is the decision this whole lesson is about.

Where it goes wrong

  • The expert's trap. "I am the user", so you stop validating and miss new perspectives. You see it when the team stops scheduling customer calls because "we already know".
  • Vocabulary without context. You know the terms but not the procedures and stakes.
  • Arrogance over curiosity. Treating the domain as solved while regulations and behaviours keep moving.
  • Endless learning. Months of reading with no decisions. Set a time-box and a "done when".
  • Not knowing the cost of a wrong answer. For AI features, this is the failure that matters most, because it is how you set the quality bar too low for the domain.

What to do next

  • Write down your domain's real constraints (regulatory, workflow, political), not just its surface needs.
  • Write a 90-day plan with a "done when" for each phase.
  • Ask one "dumb" question this week that you've been deciding around.
  • Experience the domain hands-on, not only through reading.
  • For your AI feature, write down what a confidently wrong output costs the user, and check it with someone who does the job.
  • Write the five hardest questions someone could ask about your product, and answer them. The gaps are your study list.
