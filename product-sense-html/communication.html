Lesson 05

Communication: artifacts, influence, and interviews

Picture the launch review for an AI feature. Engineering thought it was a draft assistant, sales promised customers an autopilot, and the executive sponsor expected it to cut support costs by Christmas. They all watched the same demo, and each of them heard a different product. Nobody was careless. The product's purpose was simply never written down clearly enough to survive the trip from one meeting to the next. Product sense you can't communicate can't move a team, and with AI, where nobody is quite sure what the model can do, vague communication turns uncertainty into misalignment.

Three kinds of communication matter here. Artifacts (vision, strategy, proposals) capture your thinking so others can act on it. Influence earns buy-in when you have no authority to compel it. And interviews, on either side of the table, are where your product sense is judged by how you explain it.

The loop at the right of the diagram is the part people forget. Alignment fades, so the PM ends up as what some call the team's chief repetition officer, the person who keeps saying why the product exists until everyone else can say it too.

Writing the artifacts

Artifacts are the written things a PM produces. They capture product sense in words and become the team's reference.

  • Vision. A short picture of the future you'll create, rooted in user value. Amazon's Kindle vision is the standard example: "Every book ever printed, in any language, all available in less than 60 seconds." It describes the outcome rather than the method. To write one, imagine your product wildly successful in five years, describe what that looks like for users, and distil it.
  • Strategy document. If vision is the destination, strategy is the map. It names target segments, value propositions, positioning and a few product pillars, and it says what's out. Tell it as a story (problem or opportunity, then approach, then why it wins), back it with data, and match the depth to the reader, with one page for executives and the full document for the team.
  • Proposals and one-pagers persuade people to back a specific idea through context, problem, solution and impact. Amazon's press release and frequently-asked-questions document (PRFAQ) asks you to write the launch press release first and the FAQ after it. Whatever the format, answer five questions. Who is it for, what problem does it solve, why now, how do we know it's needed, and what does success look like?

The PRFAQ habit is worth stealing even if you never write one. If you can't write the customer-facing story of the finished product in one page, you don't understand the product yet, and building won't fix that. A cheap version for any team is to draft the launch announcement at kickoff and treat every sentence you can't yet write as an open product question.

For an AI feature, write down what it won't do as carefully as what it will. Be candid about the model's limits and unknowns, because an artifact that oversells the demo is how three people end up imagining three products.

Reviewing an AI-drafted artifact

AI tools now write a plausible first draft of any of these in seconds, and reviewing one is a different skill from writing one. Matthew Wensing has a useful name for what goes wrong:

"I think of them as like micro hallucinations it's not that it's totally wrong" — Matthew Wensing, How a VP of Product Uses Claude Without Producing Slop, The Growth Podcast, around 17:33

The risk is a confident draft that quietly skips a non-goal, invents a success metric nobody agreed, or states a stakeholder's motivation you never confirmed. Read it the way you'd read an engineer's design document, checking each claim against evidence you actually have rather than evidence the draft asserts. A draft you didn't question is a draft you don't own, whoever's name is on it. (The technical-PM version of this discipline covers reading an AI-drafted PRD.)

Worked example: checking an AI-drafted one-pager

This example is invented, and so are its numbers. Say you ask a model to draft a one-pager for an AI feature that suggests replies in a customer inbox. It produces a clean page in a minute. Before you send it, you go through it sentence by sentence and mark every factual claim. There are 14.

The two small errors are typical. The draft says customers "asked for" suggested replies when your notes show they asked for faster replies, and it says the pilot ran for six weeks when it ran for four. The invented claim is a success target of a 30% cut in handling time that nobody has ever agreed. You fix the two errors, delete the target and replace it with "success metric: to be agreed with support leads", and turn the three unsupported claims into questions in an "open questions" section. Eight of the fourteen claims survived as written, so the review took twenty minutes and removed three statements that would have cost you credibility in the review meeting.

Earning buy-in without authority

A brilliant idea still needs buy-in, and a PM usually can't compel it. This lesson keeps to the basics, because Executive influence and stakeholders covers influence in depth.

Start from what each person values. An engineering lead cares about feasibility and team morale, a finance lead about return, and sales about customer demand. Ethan Evans makes the same point about the top of a company:

"understanding that lens of how does your C-suite think and how do they make decisions" — Ethan Evans, Amazon VP: The Magic Loop Framework to Get Promoted, The Growth Podcast, around 21:32

Then build credibility with data and candour. An illustrative example is "60% of 200 surveyed users were frustrated with onboarding", which is the kind of number that carries a proposal if you have it. Be honest about risks and unknowns too ("here's what we know, here's what we're still investigating"), because over-selling erodes trust and candour builds it. Wrap the idea in a story about one user's day, so people feel the problem rather than just hear it. Meet sceptics one-to-one, since a sceptic who feels heard often becomes an ally, and sometimes they've spotted a real risk.

Be wary of a meeting where everyone nods. Matthew Wensing again:

"people can feign alignment really easily" — Matthew Wensing, How a VP of Product Uses Claude Without Producing Slop, The Growth Podcast, around 38:28

So test alignment instead of assuming it. Ask each stakeholder to say in their own words what the feature is for and what it won't do. Where the answers differ, you've found the misalignment before launch day does.

Interviews, on both sides of the table

As the candidate. Product-sense questions ("design a product for X", "improve feature Y for user Z") test structured thinking, creativity, empathy and clear communication. Practise a frame. Clarify the goal and the user, surface their needs, prioritise a solution, and describe it with its trade-offs. Think out loud about why you make each choice, because that's what shows judgment. Keep a few real stories ready and structure them as Situation, Task, Action, Result (STAR).

Gayle Laakmann McDowell adds a warning about using STAR mechanically:

"So yes, use the STAR framework, but you need to be thinking in your preparation about like, what are the signals that this story can send?" — Gayle Laakmann McDowell, Cracking the PM Interview, with the author of the book Gayle McDowell, The Growth Podcast, around 42:19

For a product-sense story, the signals are usually the user insight, how you weighed the options, the principle that guided you and what you learned.

As the interviewer. Judge product sense by how candidates reason. Do they start from the user, ask about goals and pain, and offer more than one solution? Ask follow-ups ("why that approach?", "what if metric A drops?") to see their reasoning and whether they can adjust. Have them critique an existing product. Strong candidates structure the critique around user needs, cite what they observed and justify their improvements.

Where it goes wrong

  • Jargon and length. A document only its author understands, which busy stakeholders won't dig into.
  • Facts without story. A technically correct pitch that never makes anyone feel the problem.
  • Over-selling. Hiding risks to win approval, then losing trust when reality arrives.
  • Assumed alignment. Believing people remember the vision, or that nodding means agreement. It shows up at launch, as three teams describing three products.
  • Rubber-stamping the AI draft. Circulating a fluent, complete-looking artifact without checking its specifics. It's the same over-selling risk, only faster.

What to do next

  • Give your current vision or strategy document to someone outside the team. If they don't get it without you narrating, sharpen it.
  • Check your next proposal answers who it's for, what problem, why now, how you know, and what success looks like.
  • Before you send an AI-drafted artifact, mark every factual claim and check each one.
  • Ask three stakeholders to describe the feature in their own words, and compare the answers.
  • Map who cares about what, and tailor your next proposal to it.
  • Repeat the "why" often enough that the whole team can say it.
